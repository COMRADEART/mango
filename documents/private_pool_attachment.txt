SECTION 1. GEOGRAPHY OF THE PENINSULA

The Veylmark Peninsula occupies the western shore of the continent of Cindralle. Its 914-kilometre coast runs from Stravent Head in the northwest to the Talvara mole in the south, broken only by the estuary of the River Sallowmere and the roadstead of Talvara Bay. Inland, three belts of country succeed one another from west to east: the chalk maritime fringe, the moorland plateau, and the highland wall of the Dunmarrow Fells, whose summit Mount Ardvel rises to 1,436 metres. North of the fells the Vessik Uplands descend toward the continental interior. The peninsula's area is 48,210 square kilometres, and the 2021 census recorded 2.9 million inhabitants.

SECTION 2. SETTLEMENTS

Ashquill, the capital, stands on a limestone shelf above the Sallowmere estuary and held 612,000 people at the 2021 census. Its charter dates from 1288, its Lantern Tower from 1552, and its Sallowmere Viaduct from 1899. Ondrel Landing, a settlement of 240 on the northern shore of Lake Ondrel, serves the lakeside research station. Lantrow, the only village on the Isle of Kethrenne, holds 640 residents and the ferry berth. Millward Quay, 31 kilometres upriver from Ashquill, marks the tidal limit of barge traffic on the Sallowmere.

SECTION 3. INSTITUTIONS

Six institutions anchor the peninsula's civic and scholarly life. The Ashquill Municipal Archive, on Archive Row, keeps the city's records from 1288 onward in 14 kilometres of shelving. The Talvara Harbor Authority, incorporated in 1897, operates the deepwater port and maintains its Record Room of 440,000 cargo manifests. The Ondrel Institute of Limnology, founded in 1911, studies Lake Ondrel and publishes an uninterrupted series of annual reports since 1913. The Dunmarrow Fell Weather Observatory, established in 1902 at 872 metres, holds the continent's longest unbroken highland climate record. The Veylmark Institute of Standards and Measures, chartered in 1908, keeps the Ashquill standard metre bar, replica A-4. The Society for Cindralle Antiquities, founded in 1875, surveys monuments and publishes the Handbook of Cindralle Antiquities.

SECTION 5. INSTRUMENTS AND DEVICES

Five devices recur throughout the peninsula's technical literature. The hydralis barometer of 1869 compensated for latitude error in heighting and was used across the coastal triangulation. The Fell-line anemograph, installed at the weather observatory in 1902, remains calibrated and in daily service. The quartz-drum seismoscope, placed at the standards institute in 1926, has recorded regional seismic events continuously since. The Amberleaf rotary press model AR-3 of 1912 reached 12,000 impressions per hour and was licensed to four other printers. The harbor tidal observatory of 1903 still logs water levels twice daily beneath the eastern mole.
